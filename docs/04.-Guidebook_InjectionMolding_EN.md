## 2. Analysis Practice

### 1. Analysis Overview  [p.5]

#### 1.1 Background

##### 1) Process (equipment) definition and characteristics

**Definition of injection molding**

Injection molding is a plastic-molding method in which thermoplastic resin is heated to a flowing state, force-injected into the mold cavity ("cavity"), and cooled inside the mold to produce a molded product matching the cavity shape.

**Characteristics of injection molding**

1. Short molding cycle, high productivity (mass production possible).
2. Wide range of resins moldable (thermoplastic and thermosetting).
3. Automation is possible.
4. Large molded products can be produced.
5. High precision; complex shapes are feasible.

**Injection molding principle**

> *[Figure 1] Sterilizer data screenshot* (sic — the original guidebook reuses a figure caption from another dataset; the rest of the text refers to the injection-molding process)

The injection molding process is broadly divided into three phases: filling, pressurization, and compensating (holding).

[p.6]

1. **Filling Phase**
   - As the injection screw advances, resin first enters the mold cavity. The phase continues until the cavity is filled.
2. **Pressurization Phase**
   - The screw continues to advance, pressurizing the resin already in the cavity. Once filled, the screw slows but keeps moving forward — because resin is compressible, ~15% more resin can be pushed into the cavity. Compressibility can be observed by blocking the nozzle and advancing the screw: it advances quickly under pressure, then springs back when pressure is released.
3. **Compensating (Holding) Phase**
   - After pressurization the screw does not stop completely but continues to move forward slowly. Resin typically undergoes ~25% volume change as it transitions from molten to solid state. The compensation/holding phase counteracts this volume change. Since compression covers only ~15% but volume change is ~25%, the holding phase is always required.

The following 8 sub-steps constitute one cycle of the injection-molding process (Figure 2 schematizes the gate temperature and injection pressure for crystalline vs amorphous resins over one cycle):

1. Material is first dried to remove absorbed moisture, then loaded into the hopper attached to the injection molding machine. The screw inside the cylinder rotates so that raw material at the bottom of the hopper enters the cylinder and is plasticized. The cylinder is wrapped with heaters, so the resin is heated, melted and made flowable.
2. The upper mold half is fastened to the nozzle side (fixed clamping side) and the lower mold half to the ejector side (moving clamping side). The moving side advances to the fixed side, closing the mold. The mold must be clamped with sufficient force so that injection pressure does not force it open.
3. The nozzle contacts the sprue at the start of the upper-mold runner. The hydraulic cylinder advances the screw, injecting resin in the heating cylinder under high pressure so it fills the cavity uniformly. Strong clamping is required so the mold does not open under injection pressure — otherwise [p.7] flash will form on the parting surface.
4. Resin in the mold shrinks as the mold is cooled to solidify the product. If shrinkage is unchecked, dimensional accuracy and surface quality degrade — so injection pressure is held while resin is still molten ("holding pressure"). This refills the cavity to compensate for shrinkage and prevents quality loss. Holding also resists back-flow of resin out of the mold. The holding phase continues until the gate freezes and resin can no longer flow back nor be replenished.
5. While the mold is cooling, the injection screw is rotated by a hydraulic motor, drawing material from the hopper through the screw flights toward the nozzle. Frictional heat with the cylinder wall and the band heater melt the resin and move it forward. The screw retracts until a limit switch sized for the resin metering amount is pressed.
6. After plasticization the nozzle retracts and the mold opens.
7. Once open, the ejector rod on the moving side advances (hydraulic cylinder), pushing the ejector plate and ejector pins to release the part from the mold.
8. The mold closes again.

This repeats; one full sequence ①–⑧ is called one cycle.

[p.8]

> *[Figure 2] Schematic of temperature change near the gate and injection conditions for crystalline vs amorphous resin (source: Dr. Park Gyun-myeong, Korea Institute of Industrial Technology, Mold Technology Center)*

**Setting molding conditions**

Quality of a molded part is influenced by resin temperature, injection speed and pressure, and uniformity of cooling. Process behavior is also complicated by product shape, size and material. Conditions are usually set by combining the following sequence:

- Hold temperature constant, vary low-pressure (high-pressure) and low-speed (high-speed)
- Hold pressure constant, vary high-temperature (low-temperature) and high-speed (low-speed)
- Hold temperature constant, vary high-temperature (low-temperature) and high-pressure (low-pressure)

[p.9]

1. **Cylinder temperature**
   - Depends on material temperature, material grade, mold structure, and the machine. Should not be set too high for products needing surface gloss; raising it can increase impact strength but this depends on the material. Excessive temperature causes thermal degradation and flash. For thick products, cylinder temperature strongly affects flowability.
2. **Injection speed (and corresponding pressure)**
   - Speed and pressure are typically set high during primary injection. Speed is especially important for thick products. Uniform resin temperature in the cavity is needed for uniform strength and density. Injection speed and pressure relate to the cross-sectional area of the cavity flow route; gate location matters at mold-design time.
3. **Mold temperature**
   - Generally set high to reduce flow resistance and residual stress. Higher mold temperature gives better gloss; keeping cavity-core surface temperature uniform is ideal. Higher mold temperature can also cause larger shrinkage, so settings depend on material — cosmetic parts use higher mold temperature, functional/precision parts use lower mold temperature.
4. **Injection time**
   - Depends on product shape and size; determines complete filling and surface quality. For thick products, sink marks and voids must be prevented; injection time interacts with gate width, injection pressure, and material temperature. Too-short injection time produces wrinkle marks; voids reduce impact strength.
5. **Cooling time**
   - Varies with cylinder temperature, mold temperature, sprue, runner, and product thickness. Adequate cooling reduces deformation but lengthens the cycle. Too short cooling [p.10] causes post-ejection distortion, warpage, or cracking due to time-dependent density change.

##### 2) Pain points

**Process problem situation**

Many defects occur in injection molding. Solving them requires both physical and chemical understanding of the phenomenon. Causes are often entangled (multiple factors interacting), and remedying one defect can simultaneously fix another. The major defect modes and their direct causes are summarized below, along with field issues.

**Defect modes and causes**

a. **Short shot**
   - Occurs when the cavity is not fully filled. Ripple waves on the side opposite the gate are also short shots. Direct causes: poor material supply, insufficient injection pressure, premature solidification during flow, air resistance in the cavity. These causes overlap with sink marks and flow marks; sink marks and short shots can be hard to distinguish phenomenologically.

  > *[Figure 3] Defect — short shot*

[p.11]

b. **Flash**
   - Molten plastic leaks out from areas other than the mold cavity, solidifying at the parting line.

  > *[Figure 4] Defect — flash*

c. **Sink mark**
   - Surface depressions on the molded product. Typically appears at locally thick regions: the imbalance between volume decrease (from cooling the compressed resin) and volume increase (from pressure drop) is the direct cause. In principle this is the same problem as molding shrinkage — large local shrinkage produces a sink mark on the surface, or a void if it occurs internally. Sink marks can also appear as shallow ripples along other lines.

  > *[Figure 5] Defect — sink mark*

[p.12]

d. **Flow mark**
   - Concentric gloss bands or roughened surface centered on the gate. Caused by molten resin oscillating in a ripple-like pattern as it flows through the mold; areas with more mold-wall contact solidify first. Mechanism not fully understood. More likely when resin viscosity is high.

  > *[Figure 6] Defect — flow mark*

e. **Silver streak**
   - Silver-white streaks on the surface along the flow direction, traces of gas passing across the surface before solidification.

  > *[Figure 7] Defect — silver streak*

[p.13]

f. **Dull surface**
   - Poor gloss across the surface or a milky haze on clear products. Causes: resin not pressing closely enough against the mold surface; gas (water vapor, volatiles) from the nozzle condensing on the mold surface and interfering with direct contact. Excessive mold-release agent also produces it.

  > *[Figure 8] Defect — dull surface*

g. **Weld line**
   - Fine line where two flow fronts of molten plastic meet and fuse inside the mold. Unavoidable when the product has a recess, varying thickness forcing flow from thick to thin regions, or two or more gates. As a defect, the weld line is visually conspicuous due to poor fusion of the flow fronts, and strength drops significantly.

  > *[Figure 9] Defect — weld line*

[p.14]

h. **Bubble**
   - Cavity inside the molded part. Direct cause is gas (same as silver streaks): large gas release from the nozzle, or insufficient pressure on the molten resin inside the cavity.

  > *[Figure 10] Defect — bubble*

i. **Warpage**
   - Direct cause is residual stress in the molded product. Same mechanism as crazing but visible as dimensional change. Particularly problematic with polyethylene, polypropylene and similar resins that have large and non-uniform shrinkage due to varying cooling rates across thickness. Design care is essential.

  > *[Figure 11] Defect — warpage*

[p.15]

**Direct field issues at injection-molding sites**

a. Excessive defects such as short shots and gas marks
b. Difficulty diagnosing causes; long response times when defects occur
c. Insufficient root-cause elimination; lack of accumulated problem-solving know-how
d. Setting of injection Recipe conditions (material amount, temperature, pressure, speed, etc.) depends on operator experience

**Obstacles to problem solving**

- Defects only become visible after the molded part has cooled and shrunk, making it hard to stop or control the process at the moment of failure.

**Approach for overcoming this**

- Collect the main process variables of the injection machine in real time; predict defects through cause-of-defect analysis. When a defect is predicted, surface the responsible variables so the operator can react quickly, and feed this back into process control.

#### 1.2 Analysis Objectives

##### 1) Objective

Through analysis of quality issues and production-condition data in the injection process, find correlations between variables, identify the causal factors of major problems, and build a defect-prediction model and a production-condition optimization model using various ML algorithms.

##### 2) Manufacturing data — definition and introduction

| Variable role | Condition | Content |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Independent (process) | Temperature-related | Screw/cylinder, resin, mold, drying, hydraulic, ambient |
| Independent | Pressure-related | Filling pressure, holding pressure, back pressure (during metering), ejection pressure, mold-open pressure, mold-clamping pressure |
| Independent | Time-related | Filling time, holding time, cooling time, drying time |
| Independent | Speed-related | Injection speed, screw rotation speed, mold-open speed, ejection (ejecting) speed |
| Independent | Quantity-related | Metering, ejection amount, cushion amount |
| Dependent | Defect status | `Y` = good / `N` = defective (Labeled), blank (Unlabeled) |

##### 3) Expected effect of analyzing the manufacturing data

A model is provided that predicts good vs defective products from the many variables collected during the injection process.

[p.16]

##### 4) Implications — summary

Currently the injection process for automotive parts uses visual inspection by humans to sort good products, but human judgment cannot guarantee uniform quality. So we build AI models adapted to the dataset characteristics (with/without the good-product label) and use them for product sorting.

This analysis collects good-product sorting values and production-condition data from the injection process, performs processing/preprocessing, develops an AI model, and applies/verifies it in the manufacturing process. The aim is to apply big data and AI in small-and-medium enterprises and contribute substantially to quality improvement and cost reduction.

##### 3) Data (quality) preprocessing

**Purpose**

Data generated by real processes often has meaningless values, missing entries, or typos — quality is low. Even with strong analysis methodology, results suffer with low-quality data, so preprocessing is the most important stage of analysis.

We assess five data quality indices and improve them via preprocessing.

[p.25]

> *[Figure 15] Data quality indices*

**Five quality indices**

- **Completeness** — no missing entries in required fields.
- **Uniqueness** — entries must be unique, no duplicates.
- **Validity** — entries satisfy a defined valid range / domain.
- **Consistency** — structure, values and forms are consistently defined and mutually agreeing.
- **Accuracy** — reflected values match the actual entity.

> *[Figure 16] Data validity check*

[p.26]

**Detailed quality-index calculations**

- **Completeness** = ((1 − missing rate) / total samples) × 100
  1. Columns with Null > 30% are dropped (low completeness).
  2. Count missings with `isnull().sum()`.
  3. Compute the completeness index from this count.
- **Uniqueness** = ((1 − duplicate count) / total samples) × 100
  1. No column has unique values in this data → uniqueness is not evaluated.
  2. If needed, compose a composite key from `idx` and `workingtime` and use it as primary key for duplication checks.
- **Validity** — verify whether data is in valid range, matches the format, fits within the collection date, etc.
- **Consistency** = (consistency-satisfying samples / total samples) × 100. This welding dataset has no foreign-key columns → consistency not evaluated.
- **Accuracy** = (1 − (accuracy-violating samples / total samples)) × 100. This welding dataset has no correlated reference data → accuracy not evaluated.

##### 2.2.1 Supervised-learning methodology (Anomaly Detection)

**1) AI analysis model — selection rationale**

- **Approach:** Anomaly Detection
- Anomaly detection finds abnormal samples (defects) within a dataset. In most manufacturing data, abnormal samples are much rarer than normal samples; this motivates anomaly detection.

[p.27]

**What is anomaly detection?**

Anomaly detection identifies objects/records exhibiting patterns different from expectations. It is the methodology for distinguishing normal vs abnormal data. It spans classical statistics, ML, and DL. Because abnormal samples are typically scarce, anomaly detection requires learning normal patterns well and extracting features that separate abnormal from normal. It is often used in preprocessing to remove abnormal data.

**Deep-learning anomaly detection and its types**

Deep learning is a subset of ML. DL strengthens the practicality of AI/ML; classical ML usually requires hand-crafted feature extraction, while DL incorporates feature learning into the pipeline. DL handles unstructured data (documents, sound, image, video) and scales with data volume. Based on label availability, models split into **Supervised**, **Unsupervised**, and **Semi-Supervised**. Anomaly detection can be categorized along the same axis.

[p.28]

> *[Figure 18] Anomaly detection classification by label availability*

###### Supervised-learning based anomaly detection

i. **Description.** When all data — normal and abnormal — has labels, training proceeds in supervised fashion. The setup is very similar to a classification problem: learn patterns of normal/abnormal samples and decide whether a new input is normal or abnormal.

ii. **Pros / cons / mitigations**

| Pros | Cons | Mitigation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Higher accuracy than other paradigms because all data is labeled normal/abnormal. | Anomaly-detection datasets usually have far more normal than abnormal samples → data imbalance is frequent. | Data augmentation to acquire more abnormal samples, design of cost functions that penalize input/output divergence, etc. |

iii. **Representative algorithms.** Training is similar to standard ML classification/prediction. If enough labeled normal/abnormal data exists, classical ML such as **SVM**, **Random Forest**, and DL such as **DNN**, **AutoEncoder (AE)** can be used.

###### Unsupervised-learning based anomaly detection

[p.29]

i. **Description.** Obtaining labels is costly and time-consuming. Unsupervised anomaly detection assumes most of the data is normal and trains without labels. By learning intrinsic data characteristics, it separates normal from abnormal. Assumptions:

- Latent feature space distinguishes normal vs defect regions.
- Few samples are abnormal; most are normal.
- An anomaly score can be derived from intrinsic features such as distance or density.

ii. **Pros / cons**

| Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- |
| No labels needed → cost-efficient. | Hard to learn commonalities in complex, high-dimensional spaces, so good-product accuracy is hard to compute. Also sensitive to hyperparameters and data corruption. |

iii. **Representative algorithms.** **PCA** (linear, dimensionality reduction + reconstruction for anomaly detection). Deep models such as **AutoEncoder-based** approaches typically outperform classical ones for non-linear structures. An AE is a neural network that learns an efficient coding of the data; encoder maps input to internal representation, decoder maps it back.

[p.30]

> *[Figure 19] AutoEncoder structure*

Because the AE reconstructs the input as its output, the cost is called **reconstruction loss / reconstruction error**. If reconstruction error exceeds a chosen threshold, the input is classified as anomaly; otherwise normal — giving a defect/non-defect decision.

###### Semi-Supervised based anomaly detection

i. **Description.** When data is partially labeled, semi-supervised training applies. In practice, most data is unlabeled or only partially labeled. When labels are sparse OR class imbalance is so extreme that supervised approaches fail, models can be trained using **only normal samples** — defining the **decision boundary** of the normal-sample region and labeling everything outside that boundary as abnormal.

> *[Figure 20] Decision boundary learning*

[p.31]

ii. **Pros / cons**

| Pros | Cons |
| --- | --- |
| Training is possible even with only normal samples. Cost-efficient because acquiring abnormal samples is expensive. | Without precise abnormal labels, abnormal characteristics are hard to learn → lower accuracy than supervised approaches. The "outside the normal boundary = abnormal" assumption can heavily bias results; when abnormal and normal distributions overlap, abnormal samples may be misclassified as normal. |

iii. **Representative algorithms.** Classical: **One-Class SVM**. DL: **Deep SVDD (Deep Support Vector Data Description)**.

**AI methodology — implementation procedure**

- **Algorithm used:** Supervised anomaly detection — **Denoising AutoEncoder**.
- A Denoising AutoEncoder constrains the AE to learn meaningful features by **adding noise to the input** and training it to **reconstruct the noise-free original**. The model cannot trivially copy input to output, so it must find patterns. Noise can be produced via **Dropout** — randomly skipping some neurons in the input/hidden layer, which improves robustness. A DAE trained only on normal data will produce small reconstruction error on normal data and larger error on unseen defective data — enabling defect classification.

[p.32]

> *[Figure 21] Denoising AutoEncoder structure*

##### 2.2.2 Semi-supervised learning methodology

**1) AI analysis model — selection rationale**

- **Approach:** Semi-Supervised Learning applied to the injection machine data.
- In most manufacturers, total daily production and total defect count are visible, but per-item good/defective judgment is only available via expert inspection. The class variable for each item — needed for supervised learning — is costly and time-consuming to produce. So for the typical situation of many unlabeled samples, semi-supervised learning is applied to efficiently predict defect causes.

**Detailed introduction**

**What is semi-supervised learning?**

ML splits into Supervised, Unsupervised, and Semi-Supervised based on the presence of class labels.

- Supervised — uses labeled data to predict labels.
- [p.33] Unsupervised — uses unlabeled data only, leveraging features.
- Semi-supervised — uses BOTH labeled and unlabeled.

Semi-supervised is used for classification, regression, etc. It can take advantage of unlabeled data, which is much easier to collect. The aim: using both, achieve better performance than what would be obtained from labeled data alone.

**Example.** With only two labeled points (one male, one female), a model learns a dashed decision boundary that may be inaccurate (Figure 22, panel 1). Adding many gray unlabeled points (panel 2), the boundary moves toward a more realistic shape, improving classification.

**Procedure overview**

Semi-supervised can be implemented using the same AI methodologies as supervised/unsupervised. This guidebook uses four general ML algorithms: **SVM**, **Random Forest**, **Gaussian Naïve Bayes**, and a **Neural Network (DNN)**. Multiple ML models are compared to maximally exploit the data.

###### Support Vector Machine (SVM)

A classical ML algorithm for classification/regression. Training finds the **hyperplane** (decision boundary) that separates good vs defective items. The closest points of each class to the hyperplane are the **support vectors**. The distance between support vectors of the two classes is the **margin**; the model finds the hyperplane that maximizes the margin.

[p.35]

SVMs also map data to other dimensions. With raw linear mapping, the model searches for a hyperplane in the original space. Using a **kernel**, data is mapped into a higher-dimensional space, and the hyperplane is found there. Common kernels: **polynomial**, **radial basis function (RBF)**. Hyperparameters include **C** (regularization / margin softness) and **gamma** (kernel shape).

> *[Figure 23] Support Vector Machine*

###### Random Forest

A general-purpose model built on Decision Trees, frequently used for classification/regression. **Ensemble learning** — combining multiple learners — typically outperforms individual models; Random Forest is a famous example, yielding high accuracy and good generalization.

Random Forest builds many Decision Trees and aggregates their outputs. A Decision Tree is a hierarchical structure of nodes and edges; each tree learns a split function at each node to optimize per-step criteria. Trained trees produce deterministic outputs.

[p.36]

To create different trees, Random Forest uses **bagging**: split the data into slightly different subsets, train each tree on a different subset, and combine the resulting diverse trees. Hyperparameters include number of trees, max depth, and max leaves. Random Forest improves generalization and robustness but still inherits Decision Tree weaknesses — sensitivity to outliers and overfitting.

> *[Figure 24] Decision Tree*
> *[Figure 25] Random Forest composed of multiple Decision Trees*

###### Gaussian Naïve Bayes

A probabilistic classifier based on Bayes' theorem. In real data, variables are usually not independent, but Naïve Bayes makes the strong assumption of feature independence to build a conditional probability model. For continuous features, each feature is assumed to follow a **Gaussian (normal)** distribution — hence Gaussian Naïve Bayes. Despite the strong assumption, it often achieves decent performance with limited data, [p.37] and is considered efficient because parameters are estimated from observed data.

###### Deep Neural Network (DNN)

A deep stack of neural layers, trained for classification/regression. A neural network has input layer, hidden layer(s), output layer. Hidden layers receive data, apply matrix multiplications and non-linear **activation functions**, and produce outputs. Multi-layer networks can implement many functions and generally perform well.

Training uses **gradient descent**. Match between prediction and ground truth is measured by a **loss function**; training adjusts parameters to minimize the loss. A classification DNN ultimately outputs class probabilities (good vs defective). Stacking many layers lets the model learn complex, high-dimensional decision boundaries; modern research uses very deep models. Tunable elements: number of nodes, activation per node, number of layers, loss function, etc.

> *[Figure 25] Neural network example*

[p.38]

**Semi-supervised implementation**

Inside semi-supervised, the methodologies above are tailored to the injection-data situation. First, the labeled subset is used to train the ML models. Then the trained models are used to process unlabeled data. Two concepts are central:

- **Entropy minimization**
- **Pseudo-labeling**

A trained ML model can output not just a class but a **probability** per class — the **confidence**. With these probabilities we can compute **entropy**. When the model gives a high probability to one class (Figure 26), entropy is low — meaning the model is highly confident. For unlabeled data, predictions with low entropy are most likely to match the true class. Hence training prioritizes low-entropy predictions.

**Pseudo-labeling.** Treat the model's confident predictions on unlabeled data as virtual class labels and use them in subsequent training. Iteratively add new pseudo-labels (in low-entropy-first order) and merge into the labeled set — this increases training data diversity. By picking the most confident predictions first, the pseudo-labels are the most reliable. This is consistent with the original goal of semi-supervised learning: leverage unlabeled data to find a good decision boundary.

## 3. Application to a Similar "Injection Molding AI Dataset" at Other Sites

### 3.1 Manufacturing sites where this analysis can be applied

There are many molding methods. Major ones:

- **Injection molding** — melt the raw material (usually a thermoplastic), inject into a mold, cool, open the mold and eject to complete the shape.
- **Extrusion molding** — push a flowable material through a die to produce the product shape.

Different product types use different processes. However, the common mold-injection process below allows this analysis to be applied:

a. Dry the polymer, place in the hopper, add pigments / reinforcing additives per product spec; feed into the barrel where it is heated and mixed.
b. Liquid material is transported through the screw at appropriate temperature and pressure; injected through the nozzle into the mold to fill the cavity.
c. Once solidified, the mold opens, ejector pins push the part out. Steps a–c repeat rapidly.

### 3.2 Key considerations when applying this analysis to other sites

- **Individually traceable defect-data management is needed.** Injection-molded products pour out in volume, so a system that traces each item individually is mandatory, and defect-type-level data management is required.
- **Discussion and management of data format collected.** Currently, large preprocessing is needed between collected field data and AI-ready data. During collection, field operators and AI-analysis experts should communicate continuously about collection direction/format to build the right collection environment.
